San Francisco’s car-crime story changed shape after 2019

Reported break-ins dropped hard after 2019, but reported stolen-car cases stayed elevated and spread through a different set of neighborhoods.

By Olive DTU Social Data Updated April 6, 2026

Dataset

This story uses the San Francisco Police Department incident dataset from the city’s open-data portal, but only the newer 2018 to present file. I compare the six full calendar years from 2019 through 2024 and combine two pairs of subcategories: theft from parked vehicles and motor vehicle theft. That keeps the labels consistent across the full story.

The main takeaway is simple. If you only looked at one headline number, you could say vehicle-related crime went down. The more honest reading is that the problem changed shape: break-ins collapsed, especially in downtown and visitor-heavy areas, while stolen-car reports stayed well above their 2019 level and grew in several residential neighborhoods.

The citywide pattern split in two

The first chart shows the split clearly. Reported break-ins from parked vehicles dropped from 30,134 incidents in 2019 to 11,778 in 2024. That lines up with the city’s FY24 performance report, which said reported thefts from vehicles were down 57% from the previous fiscal year and at their lowest point since FY17 [2]. Motor vehicle theft moved differently: it peaked near nine thousand reports in 2023 and ended 2024 at 7,247 incidents, still well above the 5,390 cases recorded in 2019.

The geography shifted too

The map makes the change harder to dismiss as a statistical quirk. In 2019, break-ins were packed into the city’s dense commercial and visitor areas. Financial District/South Beach recorded 2,111 break-ins, Hayes Valley had 1,507, North Beach had 1,535, and Russian Hill had 1,280. By 2024, those same neighborhoods were far lower: 702, 442, 611, and 410.

That downtown contraction coincides with a city center that has not returned to its pre-pandemic rhythm. San Francisco’s office-vacancy tracker notes that vacancy was only 4.7% in the second quarter of 2019, before rising far above that level in the years that followed [1]. I cannot say vacancy caused fewer break-ins, but it is a plausible backdrop: fewer commuters, fewer parked cars, and fewer visitor-heavy targets could all change where theft opportunities appear.

At the same time, the 2024 stolen-car layer is more spread out. The biggest increases are not in Financial District/South Beach or North Beach. They show up in places like Mission, where motor vehicle theft rose from 506 to 851, and Bayview Hunters Point, where it climbed from 575 to 868. The map therefore tells a more interesting story than a simple citywide decline: the hotspot pattern moves rather than disappearing.

Neighborhoods did not move together

The interactive scatter plot combines both trends in one view. Every point is a neighborhood. Moving left means fewer reported break-ins in 2024 than in 2019. Moving upward means more reported motor vehicle theft. The most important pattern is how many neighborhoods land in the upper-left area of the chart: fewer break-ins, but more stolen cars.

Mission is the clearest example. It loses 1,281 break-ins over the period, but gains 345 motor vehicle theft reports. Bayview Hunters Point also stands out, with a smaller break-in drop of 333 paired with a large motor-theft increase of 293. Financial District/South Beach moves far left but barely moves up, which fits the idea that the sharpest decline stayed tied to the old downtown break-in geography.

This is also where the story becomes more careful. Police incident data measures reported and recorded events, not a pure underlying level of victimization. Reporting behavior, enforcement attention, vehicle technology, commuting patterns, and street activity can all change these numbers. That is why I prefer the phrasing that vehicle crime shifted rather than claiming that one type was solved or caused by a single factor.

Limitations. This story uses calendar years, while some official city reports use fiscal years, so the counts will not match perfectly. It also focuses only on reported incidents in one police dataset.
